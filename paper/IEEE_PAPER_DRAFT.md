# Transportability of Lightweight Reliability Signals for Network Intrusion Detection under Source-Only Cross-Domain Transfer

## Abstract

Reliability signals are increasingly used to identify uncertain or potentially erroneous predictions from network intrusion detection systems, but their usefulness may change when a detector is deployed in a different network environment. This study evaluates the transportability of five lightweight post-hoc reliability signals—source-calibrated confidence, Random Forest tree disagreement, feature-space distance, split conformal scoring, and Isolation Forest—under source-only cross-domain transfer. A fixed Random Forest detector is trained on either NF-UNSW-NB15-v3 or NF-CSE-CIC-IDS2018-v3 using a common frozen feature representation, and each reliability layer is evaluated in both transfer directions without target-domain adaptation, recalibration, or tuning. Ten random seeds are used for each direction and method. The primary security endpoint is attack-miss area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), where an attack miss denotes an attack flow incorrectly classified as benign. Operational utility is evaluated by the fraction of attack misses captured within fixed analyst-review budgets.

Reliability performance varies substantially with transfer direction and with the reliability mechanism. In UNSW→CIC transfer, Random Forest disagreement achieves the highest mean attack-miss AUPRC (0.238), whereas split conformal scoring performs best in CIC→UNSW transfer (0.079). At a 5% review budget, split conformal scoring captures the largest mean fraction of attack misses in both directions, reaching 17.4% for UNSW→CIC and 14.7% for CIC→UNSW. These results show that generic error detection and security-relevant false-negative prioritization are not interchangeable objectives and that no single reliability signal dominates across both transfer directions. The findings highlight the need to evaluate reliability mechanisms under the specific deployment shift and security objective for which they are intended.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Network intrusion detection systems (NIDSs) are commonly trained and evaluated using traffic collected from a particular environment, capture period, or benchmark distribution. Deployment, however, exposes the trained detector to traffic generated by different hosts, services, workloads, network conditions, and attack activity. Recent cross-dataset and temporal-shift studies show that this change can substantially alter classifier discrimination, calibration, threshold behavior, and the operational interpretation of model scores [1], [2]. These observations are important because an IDS failure is not equally costly across all prediction errors: an attack incorrectly classified as benign can remain invisible to the analyst workflow, whereas an ordinary classification error may be operationally benign.

A natural response is to place a reliability layer on top of the detector and use it to identify predictions that deserve additional scrutiny. Lightweight signals such as predictive confidence, model disagreement, feature-space distance, conformal uncertainty, and unsupervised novelty scores can be computed without replacing the underlying classifier. Recent studies have demonstrated the value—and the limitations—of such signals under distribution shift. For example, a 2026 audit of confidence-based monitoring, cross-model agreement, and conformal abstention in temporal IDS transfer found that some label-free monitoring criteria could become misleading under shift, while another 2026 study explicitly examined cross-dataset score orientation, calibrated alerting, and alert-budget behavior [1], [2]. Recent conformal work has also shown that probability-space and feature-space novelty can behave differently across unseen attack families, including cases in which the direction of a score is effectively reversed [3].

These findings establish that reliability estimation under shift is not a single problem with a universally reliable solution. The practically relevant question is narrower: **when a detector is frozen and moved to another network domain without target-domain adaptation or recalibration, which reliability signals continue to prioritize the detector's dangerous false-negative predictions?** This question differs from ordinary intrusion-classification performance. A reliability signal can be useful for identifying generic prediction errors while being poorly aligned with attacks that the detector misses, and a signal can have modest threshold-free ranking performance yet still capture a useful fraction of dangerous errors when an analyst can inspect only a small portion of the traffic. Consequently, both the security endpoint and the operational review budget must be treated explicitly.

This study evaluates that question using a fixed Random Forest detector and five lightweight post-hoc reliability mechanisms: source-calibrated confidence, Random Forest tree disagreement, exact feature-space distance, split conformal scoring, and Isolation Forest. The experiment uses NF-UNSW-NB15-v3 and NF-CSE-CIC-IDS2018-v3 within a common NetFlow-v3 feature interface [4]. The reliability mechanisms are fitted or calibrated using source-domain information only and are then frozen before target-domain scoring. Evaluation is performed in both UNSW→CIC and CIC→UNSW directions over ten random seeds. The primary security endpoint is attack-miss AUPRC, where the positive population consists of attack flows that the fixed detector incorrectly labels as benign. Operational utility is measured by attack-miss capture at fixed analyst-review budgets, with 5% as the primary budget and 1% and 10% as secondary settings.

The study makes four principal contributions:

1. **Controlled transportability evaluation:** It provides a common experimental comparison of five lightweight reliability layers under an identical fixed detector, feature interface, source-only transfer protocol, and bidirectional cross-domain evaluation.

2. **Security-specific reliability endpoint:** It evaluates reliability as a mechanism for prioritizing detector attack misses rather than treating generic prediction error as a sufficient proxy for security risk.

3. **Bidirectional and operational analysis:** It examines whether reliability rankings remain stable when the source and target domains are reversed and measures the fraction of attack misses that can be recovered within fixed analyst-review budgets.

4. **Failure-mode characterization:** It separates attack-miss ranking from generic-error ranking and examines directional score behavior, constrained family-level behavior, selective risk, calibration/conformal diagnostics, and computational cost to identify conditions under which a reliability mechanism becomes less useful or changes its operational interpretation.

The paper therefore treats reliability estimation as a transportability problem rather than as an isolated uncertainty-estimation problem. The central objective is not to identify a universal winner, but to determine whether conclusions drawn from one deployment direction, one error definition, or one operational budget remain valid after the detector is exposed to a different network domain.

## II. RELATED WORK

### A. Distribution Shift and Cross-Dataset Intrusion Detection

A substantial body of IDS research evaluates performance under changes in traffic distribution rather than assuming that training and deployment data are identically distributed. Recent work demonstrates that benchmark changes can alter not only conventional discrimination metrics but also the operating behavior of source-derived thresholds and confidence scores. Jin and Lee evaluate harmonized cross-dataset and chronological settings and report severe threshold-transfer failures, including cases of zero target recall and inverted score orientation under strict cross-dataset transfer [2]. Ahmed et al. similarly examine temporal IDS shift using label-free confidence monitoring, model agreement, and conformal abstention, showing that monitoring signals themselves can become unreliable when the deployment distribution changes [1].

Another recent line of work addresses the same deployment problem through adaptation. For example, a 2026 IEEE Access study evaluates transfer-learning strategies for cross-dataset IDS and reports improved target performance after adapting a source-trained neural model to the target setting [10]. Such studies are complementary to the present work but answer a different question. Adaptation asks how performance can be recovered after using target-domain information; the present study intentionally removes that degree of freedom and instead asks how much reliability information remains useful when the detector and reliability layer are frozen before target evaluation.

The distinction is important for deployment assessment. A source-only protocol isolates the transportability of the reliability signal itself, rather than conflating transportability with the effectiveness of a target-domain adaptation strategy. At the same time, the literature establishes that cross-domain degradation is a real concern, so a reliability study should not be interpreted as a replacement for adaptation research. Instead, it provides evidence about the failure-detection layer that would remain relevant when adaptation is unavailable, delayed, undesirable, or intentionally excluded from a deployment workflow.

### B. Confidence, Calibration, and Uncertainty for Reliable IDS

Confidence calibration is a longstanding reliability concept in machine learning. Guo et al. showed that a classifier's confidence values need not correspond reliably to empirical correctness and demonstrated that simple post-hoc calibration can substantially improve probability calibration [6]. This distinction matters in IDS because a high predictive probability can otherwise be mistaken for a guarantee that the underlying decision is trustworthy.

Recent IDS-specific work has moved beyond ordinary classification accuracy to evaluate uncertainty explicitly. Talpini et al. compare uncertainty-aware machine-learning approaches for network intrusion detection and argue that misleadingly high confidence on misclassified or unknown traffic limits IDS trustworthiness [5]. Their evaluation includes uncertainty-aware Random Forest and neural approaches, open-set detection, and active-learning implications. This line of research motivates the use of inexpensive reliability layers without requiring the detector itself to be redesigned.

The present study differs in emphasis. Rather than evaluating uncertainty primarily as a property of a classifier or as an open-set detection mechanism, it evaluates multiple existing reliability constructions as **post-hoc prioritizers of the detector's false-negative attacks after cross-domain transfer**. Confidence is therefore treated as one candidate signal among several, rather than as a sufficient proxy for reliability. This also allows direct comparison with mechanisms that capture different notions of atypicality, including disagreement among ensemble trees, feature-space distance, conformal uncertainty, and unsupervised isolation.

### C. Selective Prediction and Analyst-Centered Triage

Selective classification formalizes the idea that a predictive system may abstain on difficult examples rather than treating every prediction identically. Geifman and El-Yaniv established the reject-option formulation in which a fixed classifier can trade coverage for lower selective risk [8]. Conformal prediction extends this broader uncertainty framework by constructing prediction sets or intervals with finite-sample guarantees under appropriate exchangeability assumptions [7].

These concepts have clear operational relevance for IDS because a system may route uncertain flows to an analyst instead of attempting to automate every decision. Recent IDS studies explicitly evaluate conformal prediction and analyst triage. Arepalli et al. investigate conformal triage together with probability-space and feature-space novelty across held-out attack families and show that the behavior of different reliability signals is strongly family dependent; they also report cases in which one score is informative while another becomes effectively inverted [3]. Their study demonstrates that conformal methods can provide useful coverage control while novelty detection remains a separate and less uniformly reliable objective.

The present study adopts the operational motivation of selective prediction but uses a different primary question. Rather than asking whether a prediction set achieves a desired coverage guarantee or whether an unseen family can be identified in isolation, it asks whether a frozen reliability score can **prioritize the attacks that the detector has already missed**. Analyst review is represented explicitly through fixed review budgets, allowing the study to measure how many dangerous false negatives can be surfaced when only a small fraction of target flows can be inspected.

### D. Novelty Detection, Feature-Space Distance, and Unsupervised Signals

Feature-space distance and unsupervised anomaly detection provide reliability signals that do not depend directly on the classifier's predicted class. Nearest-neighbour distance is a simple example: a target observation can be ranked according to how far it lies from source-domain reference observations. Isolation Forest is a complementary construction that identifies observations that are easier to isolate than typical observations and was originally proposed as an efficient anomaly-detection method [9].

These methods are attractive for deployment because they can provide information about atypical inputs even when a classifier's predictive confidence is high. However, their interpretation is representation dependent. A point that is far from the source distribution in the chosen feature space is not necessarily a dangerous classifier failure, and a genuinely novel attack may remain close to common benign or known-attack traffic. Recent conformal IDS work provides concrete evidence of this distinction: probability-space and feature-space novelty can rank different unseen families in opposite directions [3].

Accordingly, the present study treats feature-space distance and Isolation Forest as candidate reliability signals rather than as generic definitions of "unknownness." Their value is measured by the same security endpoint used for the other mechanisms: whether they prioritize attack misses produced by the frozen detector.

### E. Positioning of the Present Study

The recent literature establishes that none of the individual ingredients examined here is new in isolation. Cross-dataset IDS evaluation, uncertainty estimation, conformal prediction, anomaly detection, selective prediction, score-orientation analysis, and budget-constrained alerting have all been studied independently and, increasingly, in combination [1]–[3], [5], [10]. The contribution of the present study is therefore empirical and integrative rather than algorithmic.

The study focuses on a specific controlled comparison that remains important for deployment: a single fixed RF detector, a common frozen NetFlow-v3 representation, five lightweight post-hoc reliability signals, strict source-only transfer in both directions between NF-UNSW-NB15-v3 and NF-CSE-CIC-IDS2018-v3, and attack-miss prioritization as the primary security endpoint. The study further separates threshold-free attack-miss ranking from analyst-budget capture, generic prediction error, directional behavior, and other diagnostic outcomes.

This positioning deliberately avoids claiming that reliability under distribution shift is an unexplored problem. Instead, the study asks whether existing lightweight signals remain useful for a particular security failure mode after the detector is transferred without target adaptation, and whether conclusions about their utility remain stable when the deployment direction and operational review budget change.
